# Chapter 1 — The Tailor Who Couldn't Be Heard

The shears knew the cloth before Iqbal did.

Forty-one years old, third in a line of three, and still his hands did what his
grandfather's hands had done: laid the chalk-stripe flat, smoothed it twice with
the heel of the palm, weighted the corner with the same iron cube that had held
down cloth in 1961. The French chalk went on in a line so thin you could lose it
by breathing. Then the shears — long, black-handled, heavy enough to need the
whole forearm — and that sound. That long, clean, closing sound, like a sentence
finishing properly.

It was the best sound in the world, and there was nobody in the shop to hear it.

Hameed & Sons had four chairs for waiting customers. Iqbal's grandfather had put
them there because in his day you needed four. On a Saturday in the eighties his
father had turned people away at the door. There was a photograph on the wall
above the mirror — the shop's tenth anniversary, twenty-two men crowded into a
frame, every one of them in a suit cut in this room.

This morning the four chairs held: a bolt of grey worsted, a stack of pattern
paper, a cardboard box of Asha's aprons half-finished, and nothing.

It had been nothing since Thursday.

---

Aryan came in at eleven-forty with two teas and his face already apologising.
Nineteen, quick hands, quicker eyes, three years an apprentice and still not
allowed near a lapel. He put the tea down on the corner of the cutting table
where tea was allowed to go, and then he stood there in a way Iqbal had learned
to read.

"Say it."

"It's nothing, ustad."

"You've been carrying it since the shutter went up. Say it."

Aryan took out his phone, thumbed it twice, and turned it around.

A video. A shop — no, not a shop, a *floor*, third level of the new mall, all
white light and glass, a wall of suits hanging in perfect parallel like soldiers
who had never been to war. A young man walked a slow circle in front of a mirror
while text swam up beside him: **48-HOUR SUIT. 200+ FABRICS. FREE FIRST FITTING.**
Then a price. Then a logo. Then, before Iqbal could finish being appalled, the
video started again from the top, because that is what videos do now.

"How many people have seen this?"

Aryan scrolled. Showed him the number under it.

Iqbal looked at the number for a long moment. Then he looked at his four empty
chairs. In thirty years of trading, this shop had never in a single day been
seen by as many people as that number, and the number was from Tuesday.

"Forty-eight hours," he said. "You can't rest a shoulder in forty-eight hours."

"I know, ustad."

"The cloth needs to *sit*. It needs to learn the man." He put the shears down,
which he never did mid-cut. "It's a costume. They're selling costumes."

"I know."

"So?"

Aryan didn't answer. That was the second thing, and Iqbal saw it arrive on the
boy's face before it reached his mouth.

"There's — you'll hear it anyway. Khanna-sahab's son. The wedding."

"December. Yes. He'll come after Diwali, they always—"

"He's not coming." Aryan said it fast, to get it over with. "The sherwani's
ordered. Online. It came last week. Silk, cream, the whole thing. His mother
showed my mother the box."

The shop went very quiet. Outside, a scooter argued with a truck.

Iqbal's father had cut Khanna-sahab's wedding suit. Iqbal had cut Khanna-sahab's
son's first blazer, a navy thing for a school function, and remembered the boy
squirming on the stool and the father saying *stand straight, this man is
building you*. Iqbal had that boy's measurements in the ledger. Chest, waist,
sleeve, the slightly dropped right shoulder from years of a schoolbag. He could
have turned to the shelf and found the page in under a minute.

"Did he not—" Iqbal stopped. Started again. "Did he think we had closed?"

"No, ustad."

"Then what? The price? Tell me it was the price, I can fight a price."

Aryan looked at the floor. "I think he just... didn't think of you."

---

That was the sentence that did it. Not the mall. Not the number under the video.
*He just didn't think of me.*

The shop was here. The sign was here, repainted last year in the same green.
Anyone in this lane could have walked a hundred metres and found him, and the
boy hadn't needed to look, because the boy hadn't remembered there was anything
to look for. Iqbal had spent thirty years being findable and had never once
asked whether he was being *thought of*.

He sat down in one of the four chairs, which he had also never done, and that is
where Asha found him.

---

She came in the way she always came in — sideways through the half-open door,
because she was always carrying something — and stopped dead.

Asha ran Asha's Oven, two lanes over: eleven tables, a wood counter she'd sanded
herself, and a queue on Sunday mornings that went past the chemist. Iqbal had
stitched every apron her staff had ever worn. He'd stitched them when there were
two staff and she was paying him in instalments and pretending it wasn't
instalments.

She had been in this shop maybe four hundred times. She had never seen him
sitting in a customer chair.

"Ah," she said. "There it is."

"There what is."

"That face." She set her bag down. "I know that face. I wore it for eight
months. I used to sit in my own shop at four in the afternoon with the oven off
and count the tables and think, this is the end of something my mother started."

"It's not the same."

"No," Asha agreed. "You're worse. I only had four years of history to lose. You
have three generations."

Iqbal laughed despite himself, which was probably the point.

She pulled the second chair around, moved the bolt of worsted onto the floor
with an ease that suggested she considered the chairs a scandal, and sat facing
him.

"Tell me what happened."

He told her. The video. The number. Khanna's son and the cream silk and the box
his mother had shown around the lane like a trophy.

Asha listened all the way through without once saying *but*. Then she said:

"Do you know what I did wrong for eight months? I thought the problem was my
cake."

"Your cake is—"

"My cake was the best cake in this neighbourhood. It still is. That was the
problem." She leaned forward. "I kept making it better. Better ingredients,
better icing, I did a whole month on the coffee. And nothing happened, because
nobody was arguing with my cake. Nobody had ever tasted my cake. They weren't
choosing the other place *over* me. I wasn't in the room when they chose."

"So what changed?"

"I got into the room." Asha took out her phone and put it on the cutting table,
face up, between them, like a card being played. "This."

Iqbal looked at it with open scepticism. "Photographs of buns."

"That's what I thought too. Iqbal — for eight months my problem wasn't the
baking. It was that I was a baker doing five jobs badly at eleven at night. I
had to decide *what* to say. Then *write* it. Then make it *look* like
something. Then cut it down so it wasn't four minutes of my hands. Then work out
whether any of it did anything. Five jobs. An agency has five people for those
five jobs. I had me, at eleven at night, after a fourteen-hour day."

"So you hired someone."

"I couldn't afford someone. I stopped doing the five jobs alone." She tapped the
phone. "There's an agency in here. A small one. Five staff, and they don't sleep,
and they don't send an invoice."

Iqbal's eyebrow went up, and he let it stay up.

"Laugh," said Asha. "I did. Here's the staff." She counted them off on her
fingers. "One — a **strategist**. The one who asks who you're actually talking
to and what you want them to do about it. I tell it what my week looks like, it
tells me the three things worth saying, and it makes me throw out the other
eleven. Two — a **writer**. I dictate two rambling minutes about sourdough and
it hands me back four lines that sound like me on a good day. Three — a
**designer**. Makes it look like it belongs to my shop and not to a template.
Four — an **editor**. Cuts the boring bit at the start, which is always there,
and always at the start. Five — an **analyst**. Tells me on Monday which thing
worked and why, so Tuesday isn't a guess."

"And this is—"

"A strategist, a writer, a designer, an editor and an analyst. In my apron
pocket. Next to the thermometer." She sat back. "That's the Pocket Agency. It
took me eleven days to stop feeling stupid about it and about six weeks to get
the Sunday queue."

Iqbal picked the phone up. Turned it over. It was, unhelpfully, just a phone —
scratched at the corner, flour in the seam of the case.

"Asha. I cut cloth. My father cut cloth. I don't know how to be — whatever this
is."

"You don't have to be. You have to *tell them what you already know*." She
pointed at the chalk-stripe on the table behind him. "Do you know how many
people in this city have never once watched a suit being cut? Do you know that
sound is *interesting*? You think it's Tuesday. It's a miracle to everyone who
isn't you."

---

The door swung. A man came in at speed, phone at his ear, saying "—no, no, tell
him Thursday, Thursday latest," and held up one finger to the room without
looking at it. Rajat. Regional something at a firm two buildings down, in the
shop maybe twice a year and always in a hurry.

He finished the call. "Iqbal-bhai. Conference in Hyderabad, the twelfth. I need
the grey. Can you do the twelfth?"

"I can do the twelfth."

"Good man." Then Rajat noticed the two of them sitting in the customer chairs,
and the phone on the cutting table, and he did the small delighted smile of a
person who has walked into a conversation he has opinions about. "What's all
this? Business meeting?"

"Asha is teaching me marketing," said Iqbal.

"Ah." Rajat put his phone in his pocket, which was how you knew it was serious.
"Ustad, listen to me, because I do this for a living and I don't want you to
waste your time. Marketing needs *budget*. That's the whole thing, that's the
first line of the first chapter. You need an agency. You need a media plan, you
need spend behind it, you need someone who understands reach and frequency."
He turned his hand over, generous. "This reels business — very cute for a
bakery. Genuinely. Cakes, colours, people like that. But you're a *tailor*.
Bespoke is word-of-mouth. That's a different game."

Asha, in a tone of great sweetness, said, "It was cute for the bakery."

"You know what I mean, Asha-ji."

"I do, yes."

Rajat took a card from his wallet — good stock, embossed — and set it on the
cutting table, just clear of the chalk line, because he was not a barbarian.
"My cousin runs a shop. Proper agency, twelve people. He'll do you a package. I'm
saying this as a friend, ustad: don't fiddle with a phone for six months and
then find out you needed professionals. Get it done properly the first time."

He looked at his watch, remembered Thursday, and was gone.

The door settled. The scooter outside had won its argument with the truck.

Iqbal picked up the card. Ran his thumb over the raised letters, which were, he
had to admit, very well done.

"He's not entirely wrong," said Asha, mildly. "Budget buys reach. It really
does."

"But?"

"But he's answering a question you didn't ask. He's telling you how to be
*louder*. Iqbal, Khanna's son didn't choose the mall because the mall shouted at
him. He chose the mall because when he thought the words *I need something to
get married in*, your shop wasn't in his head. It was two hundred metres from
his house and it wasn't in his head." She stood, and picked up her bag, and
looked at the four chairs with the bolt of cloth on them. "You were found, and
you were forgotten. That's the same as never being found at all."

She went to the door, then turned.

"You don't need to be louder. You need to be remembered. That's cheaper, and
it's slower, and it's the only thing that lasts. My aprons by Friday?"

"By Thursday."

"Show-off," said Asha, and left.

---

Iqbal stood alone in his grandfather's shop.

He put Rajat's card in his shirt pocket, because you don't throw away a card, and
because some part of him wanted the option, and because he was not yet a man who
had decided anything.

Then he went back to the cutting table. Squared the chalk-stripe. Set his hand
where the line began.

And with the other hand — feeling ridiculous, feeling forty-one and third of
three and watched by twenty-two men in a photograph — he propped his phone
against the iron cube, tapped record, and let the shears make that long clean
closing sound for somebody other than himself.

## Asked

> **"Can a phone really replace a marketing agency?"**

## The One-Minute Answer: The Pocket Agency

No — and that's the wrong question. A phone doesn't replace an agency. It
replaces the *reason you needed one*.

You never wanted an agency. You wanted five jobs done, and you couldn't do all
five at eleven at night after a fourteen-hour day. That's what an agency actually
sells you: five people who do five things you can't do alone.

You now have all five. They live in your phone, they work at midnight, and they
don't invoice:

| The staff | What they do for you |
|---|---|
| **The Strategist** | Decides who you're talking to and what you want them to do. Kills the eleven ideas so the three can live. |
| **The Writer** | Turns your rambling voice note into four lines that sound like you on a good day. |
| **The Designer** | Makes it look like it came from your shop, not from a template. |
| **The Editor** | Cuts the boring first eight seconds. There are always boring first eight seconds. |
| **The Analyst** | Tells you on Monday what worked, so Tuesday isn't a guess. |

What the phone *can't* do is know your trade. It has never heard your shears. It
doesn't know that a shoulder needs to sit, or which regular takes his tea without
sugar, or why the December wedding season starts in September. That part was
never the agency's job either — agencies were always just asking you for it in a
meeting and charging you to type it up.

So: the phone doesn't replace the agency. **You** were always the agency's most
important input. The phone replaces the other five people, the six-week timeline,
and the invoice.

Rajat isn't lying when he says budget buys reach. He's answering a different
question. Budget makes you *louder*. It does nothing about being *forgotten* —
and being forgotten is what's actually emptying your chairs.

## The Principle

> ### Found but forgotten is the same as never found.
>
> Khanna-sahab's son knew exactly where Hameed & Sons was. He could have walked
> there in ninety seconds. He didn't choose against Iqbal — he never held an
> election. When the thought *I need something to get married in* arrived, the
> shop simply wasn't in the room.
>
> Being findable is a map problem, and you solved it decades ago by having a
> door. Being remembered is a mind problem, and it is the only one left.
> Discoverability without recall is a shop with the lights on and nobody
> thinking of it.

## Scene Plate

**Title:** *Empty Chairs, Eleven-Forty*

Iqbal alone at the cutting table, shot from slightly behind and to the left so we
read the room over his shoulder. Long black-handled shears in his right hand,
mid-cut, the chalk-stripe wool laid flat under an iron weight. Dust motes hanging
in a hard slab of window light — the only bright thing in the frame — falling
across the cloth and the backs of his hands.

Behind him, out of focus but unmistakable: four empty waiting chairs, one holding
a bolt of grey worsted, one holding a cardboard box of half-finished aprons. On
the wall above the mirror, the tenth-anniversary photograph, twenty-two men in
suits, too small to make out faces but clearly *full*.

The composition should put the fullness in the past (photograph, high on the
wall, in shadow) and the emptiness in the present (chairs, low, in light). Warm
greens and browns; the light doing all the work. No phone visible yet. He is
absorbed, skilled, and completely unwitnessed.

**Illustration brief:** A single phone, drawn in outline, standing upright. Its
screen is not a screen — it is the front of a small shop, with an awning, a
hand-painted sign reading **THE POCKET AGENCY**, and a lit doorway at the bottom
of the frame. Through the glass, five tiny figures at five tiny desks, each
labelled in a neat shop-signage hand: **STRATEGIST**, **WRITER**, **DESIGNER**,
**EDITOR**, **ANALYST**. Each is mid-task — one at a board, one at a typewriter,
one at a drawing table, one at a splicing bench, one at a ledger.

The whole thing sits in the cupped palm of a hand at the bottom of the frame,
scaled small enough that the joke lands: an entire agency, staffed and lit and
open for business, the size of the thing already in his shirt pocket next to
Rajat's card.
